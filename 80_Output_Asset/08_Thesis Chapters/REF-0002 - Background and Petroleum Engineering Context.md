# 2. Background and Petroleum Engineering Context

The performance of nanoparticle-stabilised energised foams under HPHT conditions depends on complex interactions between reservoir conditions, fluid formulation, rheological behaviour, and engineering requirements. Understanding these relationships provides the foundation for evaluating their application in hydraulic fracturing and identifying the limitations that motivate this research.

## 2.1 Hydraulic Fracturing and the Role of Energised Foams

The exploitation of unconventional reservoirs and mature fields presents operational and environmental challenges for conventional water-based hydraulic fracturing. Water-based stimulation requires substantial volumes of freshwater, while fluid invasion may contribute to clay swelling and formation damage, potentially reducing reservoir productivity in water-sensitive formations (Gonzalez Perdomo and Wan Madihi, 2022). Consequently, energised fracturing foams incorporating compressible gases such as nitrogen or carbon dioxide have emerged as alternative fluids for water-sensitive and under-pressured reservoirs (Wanniarachchi et al., 2017).

Energised foams can reduce water consumption while providing rheological properties that support proppant suspension and transport (Fei et al., 2018; Yekeen et al., 2018). Their engineering performance, however, depends strongly on maintaining suitable apparent viscosity and foam stability during fluid preparation, pumping, fracture propagation, and proppant placement. These properties are sensitive to interacting factors including pressure, temperature, foam quality, shear conditions, salinity, and chemical formulation. The principal engineering challenge is therefore not merely to generate a stable foam under controlled laboratory conditions, but to understand and predict how its rheological and stability characteristics change across the demanding and dynamic conditions of HPHT fracturing operations.

## 2.2 Foam Rheology under HPHT Conditions

The rheological behaviour of energised foams is strongly influenced by pressure, temperature, and shear conditions. These factors affect bubble structure, liquid drainage, apparent viscosity, and foam stability. Jing et al. (2019), for example, showed that increasing pressure alters gas-phase behaviour and bubble size, with corresponding effects on foam viscosity. Elevated temperatures have also been associated with faster liquid drainage and reduced viscosity and stability (Hutchins and Miller, 2005; Fu and Liu, 2021).

Although these studies provide important insights, their findings are often specific to particular formulations and experimental conditions. Fu and Liu (2021) and Lv et al. (2015), for example, examined foam behaviour at temperatures up to approximately 72°C and 90°C, respectively. While valuable, these experimental ranges do not capture the more extreme temperatures or the full range of coupled pressure and temperature conditions that may be encountered in HPHT reservoirs, which have been classified in previous literature as having temperatures above 150°C and pressures exceeding 69 MPa (Gonzalez Perdomo and Wan Madihi, 2022). Similarly, studies that vary pressure or temperature independently may not fully capture how these variables interact with foam quality, shear rate, salinity, and chemical formulation under reservoir conditions (Sinha et al., 2019).

The literature therefore establishes that operating conditions strongly influence foam rheology and stability, but the evidence remains fragmented across different formulations, experimental methods, and operating ranges. This limits direct comparison between studies and makes it difficult to generalise observed relationships across broader HPHT fracturing conditions.

## 2.3 Foam Quality and Rheological Models

Foam quality, expressed as the volumetric gas fraction, strongly influences foam structure and rheological behaviour. Increasing foam quality can increase apparent viscosity up to a critical range, beyond which rheological behaviour may change as the foam structure becomes increasingly gas-dominated (Akhtar et al., 2018). To describe the resulting non-Newtonian and shear-thinning behaviour, studies have applied rheological models such as the Power-law, Bingham plastic, and Herschel–Bulkley equations (Ahmed et al., 2017).

Although these models provide useful representations of foam behaviour within their calibration ranges, their empirical parameters may not remain transferable across different formulations and operating conditions. The complex and heterogeneous structure of high-quality foams further complicates representation using simplified constitutive relationships (Princen and Kiss, 1989). More recently, machine learning approaches, including XGBoost and Artificial Neural Networks, have been applied to capture nonlinear relationships in foam rheological data (Al-Darweesh et al., 2024). However, where the models examined provide only deterministic point predictions, uncertainty arising from sparse data, experimental variability, and extrapolation beyond the training range remains unquantified. This limits confidence in their application to engineering decisions under variable HPHT conditions (Zhang et al., 2025).

## 2.4 Nanoparticle–Surfactant Interactions

To improve foam stability under elevated temperatures, silica nanoparticles have been used to form interfacial barriers that enhance film elasticity and delay liquid drainage (Gonzalez Perdomo and Wan Madihi, 2022). Their stabilisation efficiency, however, depends strongly on interactions with the selected surfactant. For instance, combining negatively charged silica nanoparticles with cationic surfactants such as CTAB can induce strong electrostatic attraction, producing nanoparticle aggregates or “corks” that obstruct drainage channels (Tran et al., 2023). Studies have also reported higher apparent viscosity and stability for some cationic surfactant–nanoparticle systems than for comparable anionic mixtures (Tran et al., 2025).

This stabilising effect is highly concentration-dependent. Tran et al. (2022) reported that exceeding an optimal cationic surfactant concentration can induce excessive nanoparticle flocculation, causing particles to remain or precipitate in the bulk phase rather than stabilising the interface, with a corresponding decline in foam stability. These nonlinear and formulation-specific interactions create an important predictive challenge because relationships observed within one concentration range may not remain valid beyond it. Accurately identifying such thresholds is therefore important for predicting foam performance and defining candidate operating windows.

## 2.5 Proppant Transport and Engineering Performance

The effective suspension and transport of proppant into the fracture network are important engineering functions of energised fracturing foams. Compared with conventional slickwater, foams can provide rheological and elastic properties that support improved proppant suspension and distribution under certain conditions (Fei et al., 2018). Proppant settling is also influenced by fluid viscosity, making the maintenance of suitable rheological behaviour important for effective transport (Goel et al., 2002).

A critical limitation, however, is that elevated temperatures can degrade foam stability and compromise proppant suspension. Lv et al. (2015), for example, reported substantial increases in proppant settling velocity under elevated-temperature conditions. Because proppant transport is a downstream engineering consequence influenced by foam rheology and stability, uncertainty in predicting these primary properties may propagate into subsequent assessments of proppant behaviour and fracture performance (Tran et al., 2025). This reinforces the need to quantify predictive uncertainty when identifying candidate operating windows for further laboratory and engineering evaluation.

## 2.6 Critical Synthesis of the Existing Literature

The literature has established important relationships between HPHT conditions, foam quality, nanoparticle–surfactant interactions, rheological behaviour, and proppant transport (Gonzalez Perdomo and Wan Madihi, 2022; Tran et al., 2025). Conventional rheological models provide useful descriptions within defined experimental conditions (Ahmed et al., 2017), while data-driven approaches offer additional capacity to capture complex nonlinear relationships (Al-Darweesh et al., 2024). However, the evidence remains fragmented across different formulations, experimental methods, operating ranges, and measured outputs, limiting direct comparison and broader generalisation.

This fragmentation is compounded by small, formulation-specific datasets and limited coverage of coupled HPHT conditions (Sinha et al., 2019). As a result, models calibrated or trained on particular datasets may perform well within the conditions represented but provide limited evidence of reliability under sparse, unfamiliar, or extrapolated conditions. Where predictions are deterministic, uncertainty arising from experimental variability, limited data coverage, and model limitations may also remain unquantified (Ogbidi and Oteh, 2023).

The central challenge is therefore not simply the limited application of uncertainty-aware machine learning to foam rheology. Rather, existing approaches do not yet adequately integrate HPHT foam behaviour, predictive uncertainty, and engineering interpretation within a framework capable of supporting candidate operating-window identification. Addressing this limitation is important because fracturing-fluid design requires not only predictions of apparent viscosity and foam stability, but also an understanding of their reliability and implications for downstream engineering assessment (Tran et al., 2025).
