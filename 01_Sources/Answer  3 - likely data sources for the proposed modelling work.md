### **The likely data sources for the proposed modelling work**

The success of this research depends on obtaining high-quality experimental and field data that accurately represent **HPHT energised foam behaviour** under reservoir conditions. I expect the modelling work to use a combination of four main data sources.

**First, laboratory-generated HPHT experimental data** will be the primary source. These data are obtained using HPHT rheometers and foam evaluation equipment, where foams are tested under controlled pressure and temperature conditions. The datasets would typically include input variables such as pressure, temperature, shear rate, foam quality, surfactant concentration, nanoparticle concentration, salinity, gas type (CO₂ or N₂), and gas-liquid ratio. The corresponding outputs would include apparent viscosity, foam stability or half-life, bubble size distribution, yield stress, and fluid-loss characteristics.

**Second, published datasets from the scientific literature.** Many peer-reviewed journals provide experimental data on HPHT foam rheology, including publications in journals such as:

- _SPE Journal_
    
- _Journal of Petroleum Science and Engineering_
    
- _Fuel_
    
- _Energy & Fuels_
    
- _Journal of Natural Gas Science and Engineering_
    

These studies provide valuable experimental results that can be compiled into a larger dataset for machine learning.

**Third, industry and research collaboration data.** Since this project is industry-oriented, I expect that the University of Adelaide and the supervisory team may have access to proprietary laboratory or field datasets through collaborations with petroleum companies or research organisations. These datasets are often more representative of real reservoir conditions and would greatly improve model validation.

**Finally, field operational data**, where available. This may include hydraulic fracturing treatment reports containing reservoir pressure and temperature, injection rate, pumping pressure, foam quality, proppant concentration, fluid composition, production response, and post-treatment performance. Although field data can be noisy and incomplete, they are valuable for testing how well the developed models generalise to real engineering applications.

One challenge I anticipate is that **HPHT foam datasets are relatively small and heterogeneous**, because experiments are expensive and are often conducted under different laboratory protocols. To address this, I would:

- standardise units and measurement methods,
    
- perform data cleaning and feature engineering,
    
- carefully handle missing values,
    
- use robust cross-validation,
    
- apply uncertainty quantification,
    
- and validate the models on independent datasets to ensure reliability.
    

Overall, I see the project using **laboratory experiments as the primary data source**, **published literature and industrial datasets to expand the database**, and **field data for validation**. Combining these sources will enable the development of machine learning models that are both scientifically rigorous and practically useful for predicting HPHT foam rheology and supporting hydraulic fracturing-fluid design.
